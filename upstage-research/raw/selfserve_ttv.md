New Developer Platform That’s Self-Service\n\n![How to Launch a New Developer Platform That's Self-Service](https://blog.moesif.com/images/posts/header/how-to-launch-a-new-developer-platform-thats-self-service.png)\n\nEnterprise software companies sell very differently today than just a decade ago. Previously, most software was shrink-wrapped, required a lot of effort to distribute and implement, and was sold to an executive who would have to deploy it throughout their department or organization. The buying process involved a long sales cycle and often included pilots, cost-benefit analyses, procurement, and legal reviews.\n\nToday, enterprise companies are building developer platforms where an individual developer can sign up for a SaaS service, integrate the SDK, and start paying via a credit card in a matter of minutes. Everything from payments and telecommunications like Stripe and Twilio to e-commerce search like Algolia can now be delivered via an API and SDK. With the rise of SaaS, enterprise companies are disrupting new industries by taking a page out of the go-to-market playbook for the latest generation of mobile focused and web 2.0 companies like Facebook, AirBnB and Uber. This means ads, onboarding experience, and self-service is taking a higher priority over field or inside sales. The transition is not easy though. This article lays out some of the key elements required.\n\n## Self-service onboarding\n\nAny developer platform or self-service SaaS must start with their onboarding flow. Whereas the previous generation of enterprise companies could simply rely on a “Schedule Demo” or “Contact Us” form, developer platforms must now enable everything from sign up, to paying for the implementation via a no-touch or low-touch model. Because many enterprise software products require engineering expertise to set up and are more complicated than signing up for Netflix, a large emphasis should be placed on your onboarding flow. You’ll want to gather some information such as the company name and the role of the person. This enables the experience to be personalized - is the person is a developer or non-developer. You’ll want to clearly define the next steps for implementation, whether that’s installing an SDK or authenticating with a third party tool.\n\n### Measuring onboarding success\n\nTime To First Hello World (TTFHW), or sometimes called Time to First API Call, is a key metric to track the effectiveness of your onboarding. Specifically, TTFHW is the time it takes for a developer to sign up for your service and make their first API call. Some products may have a short TTFHW in the range of minutes, but others may be weeks or even months if serious investigation is needed. Keep an eye out for any user segments that exhibit a better TTFHW than others. For example, which marketing channel is the most effective, what integrations are they using, etc. Their first API call could be as simple as a test from Postman or from their production code. For more info, read [Best practices for Developer Relations Programs to measure success of an API platform](/blog/developer-relations/measure-success/Best-practices-for-Developer-Relations-Programs-to-measure-success-of-an-API-platform/)\n\n### Reducing onboarding friction\n\nTo reduce friction, keep the onboarding flow to around three steps or less. Even if your platform has a large surface area or large integration component, identify what is the minimum integration required to demonstrate value to the developer as quick as possible. This means less critical steps such as setting up custom rules or configuring app settings should not be shown during onboarding. For example, at Moesif we only prompt our new customers to integrate the middleware SDK to get instant visibility into their APIs. Moesif has additional SDKs to enrich the user profiles with demographic info, like plan quota or company name, but we don’t prompt users to install those during our onboarding. Instead, we nudge our users via progress checklists and drip emails. When a user redirects to an area of the product not set up, then we display the relevant secondary onboarding page. This practice is sometimes called *gradual onboarding*.\n\n## Self-service pricing\n\nSelf-service should imply no- or low-touch sales and almost always include a free tier or a limited-duration trial. If someone from your team has to be on the phone to demo the product or provision a new account, and involve engineering for implementation, then self-service will be extremely hard to pull off. Instead, simply share your enterprise pricing publicly, as the cost of acquisition will far exceed the lifetime value of the customer. Similarly, self-service is not ideal for high *initial* contract values. While a customer who was initially self-service can increase their usage over time and mature into more expensive plans, they may not have been willing to initially pay for a higher plan. Don’t launch a developer platform that’s self-service just to reduce cost of sales and support effort. Instead, view self-service as a way to open new markets that are too expensive for traditional sales (such as smaller businesses), or reduce the effort required for demand generation (such as in markets with a large user base that can be driven by word-of-mouth).\n\n### Ways to price\n\nWhile some companies can garner higher rates, most self-service models do best when the price point is less than $1,000/month, or even less than $500/month. Ideally, you want multiple price points that target a variety of customer profiles and sizes. This can be done through usage based, number of seats, multiple *tiers* or bundles, add-ons, or a combination of the above. Pick only one pricing model to prevent decision fatigue for customers. Mixing tiered plans with usage based and seats can confuse the customer.\n\n### What to price on\n\nIn order to scale with customers, many API first companies have some sort of usage-based pricing connected to a value metric. This can be transaction volumes (such as SMS sent), monthly active users, dollar volume, etc. If different customers care about different value metrics, you may need more than one in order to capture the full market.\n\nAuth0, a login API, is a clear example of this. Besides company size, they have two types of customers: B2C and B2B. B2C has very high monthly active user counts. Auth0 may appear extremely expensive if they only offered 100 or 1,000 MAU in their lower tiers; We’ve seen 1,000 MAU for invite-only beta apps, that aren’t even launched yet; 7,000+ MAU might be more appropriate for their free plan.\n\nMany B2B companies can be in the $10’s to $100’s of millions of annual run rate, without ever breaching 7,000 MAU. In order to capture value at this end of the spectrum, Auth0 decided to package certain high-value features, such as enterprise sign-in support and advanced role based access control, in their B2B plan. More information on pricing can be found in [The Anatomy of SaaS Pricing Strategy](https://www.priceintelligently.com/hubfs/Price-Intelligently-SaaS-Pricing-Strategy.pdf)\n\n### Data-driven pricing\n\nOnce you decide on the packaging and positioning of your self-service platform, you still have to determine actual limits. Does the free tier have 10,000 API transactions per month or does the free tier include 1 million API transactions per month? The best way to determine these amounts is first decide on the price points based on your customer profiles such as free, $100/month, and $500/month. Then, use your tool of choice to cluster users based on their usage data. A simple quick visual glance even scripted k-means will do. Can you find a local maxima in certain groups? You may find trends such as majority of companies with less than 10 employees have around X usage. In order to do this, you need to track usage data per customer and be able to visualize that easily through an API analytics tool like [Moesif](https://www.moesif.com/) or alternative analytics for API platforms.\n\n## Self-service support\n\nThird-party developers hate emailing or getting on the phone with someone just to resolve a support issue. In addition, solutions engineers, consultants, and engineers are very expensive as first line support. They should be available if a developer cannot find their answer online. This means you need to put effort in documentation, tutorials, GitHub examples, and other self-service resources. Empower the developer to research and resolve their own problem, rather than relying on expensive first-tier support staff. Software Delivery Performance, Developer Experience (DevEx), and Product Improvement\n\n> “If you can’t measure it, you can’t improve it – so let’s stop guessing and start tracking what really makes platforms successful, deployments faster, developers happier, and products better!”\n\n[Effective platform engineering](https://meshcloud.io/en/blog/ultimate-guide-platform-engineering/ \"Effective platform engineering\") is not just about building robust systems — it’s about driving widespread adoption, ensuring operational efficiency, and cultivating a positive developer experience. By measuring platform adoption, teams can track real usage, streamline onboarding, and enhance self-service capabilities; without this insight, even the most advanced internal platforms may remain underutilized and inefficient.\nKey performance metrics, such as deployment frequency and failure rates, offer critical feedback on the platform’s ability to support fast, stable operations, ensuring that infrastructure remains robust even under heavy demand. Equally important, developer experience metrics capture how well tools and workflows are designed to reduce cognitive load and foster seamless collaboration, ultimately driving engagement and operational excellence.\nBy systematically monitoring adoption, performance, and developer experience, platform engineering teams can continuously refine their internal platforms, remove friction, and build a high-impact engineering culture that accelerates efficiency and satisfaction across the organization.\n\n### 1. Platform Adoption\n\n**Instruction: Ensure your platform’s adoption is continuously monitored by tracking user engagement, onboarding speed, and support responsiveness to identify and resolve barriers to widespread use.**\n\n**Target Group:** Platform Engineers, DevOps Teams, Engineering Leaders, and Customer Success Managers\n\n### Metrics Table\n\n| KPI | Example |\n| --- | --- |\n| Active Platform Users and Teams | Count of users/teams actively engaging with the internal developer platform. |\n| Time to First Service | Duration it takes a new user or team to deploy their very first service on the platform. |\n| Service and Resource Consumption | Measurement of how many applications, environments, or resources are actively used. |\n| Service Adoption | Number of teams actively using platform services for deployments and operations. |\n| Support Requests & Issue Resolution Time | Volume and resolution time of support tickets, indicating platform stability. |\n\n### Detailed Explanations Platform Adoption\n\n* **Active Platform Users and Teams**\n\n  + Tracking the number of active users and teams engaging with the platform provides a high-level view of adoption.\n  + A growing number of users indicates strong adoption and increased reliance on the platform.\n  + A decline may signal usability issues or the need for better internal advocacy.\n  + Measuring engagement over time helps track long-term adoption trends.\n* **Time to First Service**\n\n  + This metric measures how long it takes a new user or team to deploy their first service on the platform.\n  + A shorter time indicates a smooth onboarding process, while delays may highlight friction in the platform experience.\n  + A quick time to first service suggests an intuitive, well-documented, and self-service-friendly platform.\n  + Longer onboarding times may indicate unclear documentation, complex configurations, or tool fragmentation.\n* **Service and Resource Consumption**\n\n  + This metric measures how many applications, environments, or infrastructure resources are actively deployed on the platform.\n  + Increased resource consumption shows deeper adoption and reliance on the platform.\n  + A mismatch between high user counts and low resource usage may suggest hesitation in fully embracing the platform.\n  + It helps track the balance between platform capabilities and actual business needs.\n* **Service Adoption**\n\n  + Service adoption tracks how many teams actively use platform services for deployments, infrastructure provisioning, and operations.\n  + High adoption indicates strong usability and perceived value of the platform.\n  + Low adoption suggests a lack of awareness, trust, or usability gaps.\n  + This metric helps assess whether the provided services align with developer needs.\n* **Support Requests & Issue Resolution Time**\n\n  + Tracking the number of support tickets and their resolution time helps measure platform stability and usability.\n  + A high ticket volume may indicate poor documentation, confusing workflows, or frequent platform issues.\n  + Fast resolution times suggest effective support and incident management.\n  + Monitoring common issues can help identify recurring problems that need long-term fixes.\n\n---\n\n### 2. Software Delivery Performance\n\n**Instruction: Focus on streamlining your software delivery process by optimizing release frequency, reducing bottlenecks, and ensuring rapid recovery from issues.**\n\n**Target Group:** Platform Engineers, DevOps Teams, and Software Engineering Leaders\n\n### Metrics Table\n\n| Metric | Example |\n| --- | --- |\n| Deployment Frequency (DF) | How often software is successfully deployed (e.g., multiple deployments per day indicate an agile, automated pipeline). |\n| Lead Time for Changes (LTC) | Time from code commit to production release, highlighting CI/CD efficiency. |\n| Change Failure Rate (CFR) | Percentage of deployments that result in failures requiring remediation. |\n| Mean Time to Recovery (MTTR) | Duration needed to restore service after an incident, reflecting incident response efficiency. |\n| SLA Compliance & Downtime Impact | Monitoring if service levels are met and the impact of downtime on productivity. |\n\n### Detailed Explanations Software Delivery Performance\n\n* **Deployment Frequency (DF)**\n\n  + Measures how often an organization successfully releases software into production.\n  + High-performing teams deploy multiple times per day, while lower performers may release only monthly or less frequently.\n  + Frequent deployments indicate agile, efficient, and automated pipelines.\n  + Organizations should prioritize reducing manual processes and increasing automation to improve DF.\n* **Lead Time for Changes (LTC)**\n\n  + Assesses how quickly a committed code change reaches production, including coding, testing, and deploying.\n  + Shorter lead times indicate an efficient CI/CD pipeline that enables faster delivery of features and fixes.\n  + Long lead times can signal bottlenecks in development, testing, or approval processes that need optimization.\n* **Change Failure Rate (CFR)**\n\n  + Measures the percentage of deployments that cause failures requiring remediation.\n  + A low CFR suggests stable releases with minimal disruptions.\n  + A high CFR indicates poor testing, inadequate monitoring, or insufficient rollback strategies.\n  + Improving CFR involves enhancing testing coverage, implementing automated QA, and ensuring reliable deployment processes.\n* **Mean Time to Recovery (MTTR)**\n\n  + Quantifies how long it takes to restore service after an incident or failure.\n  + Faster recovery times demonstrate strong incident response capabilities, minimizing downtime and user impact.\n  + Achieving a low MTTR requires robust monitoring, automated rollback mechanisms, and efficient incident resolution workflows.\n* **SLA Compliance & Downtime Impact**\n\n  + Monitors whether service levels are met and the impact of downtime on productivity.\n  + Ensuring that platform services meet agreed SLAs is critical for long-term trust and adoption.\n  + Frequent SLA breaches indicate performance or reliability gaps.\n  + Prolonged downtime directly affects developer productivity and user satisfaction.\n\n---\n\n## 3. Developer Experience (DevEx)\n\n**Instruction: Ensure your development teams thrive by continuously assessing their well-being, productivity, and collaboration, and by refining tools and processes accordingly.**\n\n**Target Group:** Platform Engineers, DevOps Teams, and Software Engineering Leaders\n\n### Metrics Table\n\n| Metric | Example |\n| --- | --- |\n| Satisfaction and Well-Being (SPACE – S) | Survey scores reflecting developers’ happiness and engagement with work and tools. |\n| Performance (SPACE – P) | Outcome-based metrics like bug resolution time and feature adoption that reflect work impact. |\n| Activity (SPACE – A) | Quantitative counts such as commits, pull requests, and reviews indicating work volume. |\n| Communication & Collaboration (SPACE – C) | Metrics like review response time and onboarding effectiveness showing team coordination. |\n| Efficiency and Flow (SPACE – E) | Cycle time and work handoffs demonstrating how smoothly work progresses. |\n| Developer Joy | An overall measure of how happy and engaged developers are with their tools, processes, and culture. |\n| Developer Feedback & Satisfaction (Platform) | Regular surveys and feedback loops capturing developers’ views on platform usability. |\n\n### Detailed Explanations DevEx\n\n* **Satisfaction and Well-Being (SPACE – S)**\n\n  + Reflects how developers feel about their work, tools, and team environment.\n  + A happy and engaged developer is more productive and less prone to burnout.\n  + High levels of burnout can indicate inefficiencies in workload management.\n  + Surveys and feedback loops are effective in gauging developer satisfaction, and organizations that prioritize well-being often see better retention and innovation.\n* **Performance (SPACE – P)**\n\n  + Focuses on the outcome of work rather than the volume of output.\n  + A developer’s contribution should be evaluated based on impact and effectiveness, not just code quantity.\n  + Metrics such as bug resolution time, feature adoption, and customer feedback provide better insights.\n  + Evaluating performance at individual, team, and organizational levels ensures a balance between speed and quality, leading to long-term software stability.\n* **Activity (SPACE – A)**\n\n  + Measures the amount of work performed, such as coding, pull requests, and reviews.\n  + However, higher activity does not always indicate higher productivity.\n  + Simple activity metrics (e.g., commits, pull requests) can be misleading.\n  + Measuring time spent on meaningful work (e.g., problem-solving, feature development) is more valuable.\n  + Engineering teams should track bottlenecks in development pipelines rather than relying solely on raw activity numbers.\n  + Activity data should always be analyzed in conjunction with other SPACE dimensions.\n* **Communication & Collaboration (SPACE – C)**\n\n  + Assesses how effectively teams coordinate, share knowledge, and work together.\n  + Effective teams rely on transparency and efficient knowledge sharing.\n  + Metrics such as review response time, onboarding effectiveness, and cross-team dependencies help capture this dynamic.\n  + Strong collaboration reduces friction in development processes, ultimately improving delivery speed.\n* **Efficiency and Flow (SPACE – E)**\n\n  + Refers to how smoothly work progresses with minimal interruptions.\n  + Developers need focused time to produce high-quality software without unnecessary context switching.\n  + Achieving a flow state improves deep work and cognitive performance, while frequent interruptions (e.g., excessive meetings) can degrade productivity.\n  + Organizations should track cycle time, lead time, and work handoffs to optimize processes, and robust CI/CD pipelines can help maintain efficiency by reducing delays in deployment.\n* **Developer Joy**\n\n  + Traditional productivity metrics often fail to capture what truly makes developers effective.\n  + Instead of asking, “How do we measure developer productivity?”, the focus should be on “How do we make developers happier?”\n  + Developer Joy emphasizes the intersection of developer experience (DevEx) and engineering culture.\n  + By providing the right tools, reducing cognitive load, and fostering a supportive engineering culture, companies can enhance developer engagement, innovation, and overall productivity.\n* **Developer Feedback & Satisfaction (Platform)**\n\n  + Involves collecting regular surveys and feedback loops regarding platform usability, performance, and support.\n  + This ongoing feedback helps identify pain points, informs continuous improvement efforts, and ensures that the platform evolves in line with developer needs.\n\n---\n\n## 4. Self-Service Maturity\n\n**Instruction: Enhance your platform’s autonomy by streamlining self-service features, ensuring rapid onboarding, and standardizing processes to reduce dependency on manual interventions.**\n\n**Target Group:** Platform Engineers, DevOps Teams, Developer Experience (DevEx) Leaders, and Engineering Managers\n\n### Metrics Table\n\n| Metric | Example |\n| --- | --- |\n| Self-Service Adoption Rate | Percentage of deployments/configurations completed without manual intervention. |\n| Time to Cloud | Duration from idea to fully deployed cloud service, showing onboarding and provisioning efficiency. |\n| Developer Satisfaction (Self-Service) | Satisfaction ratings focused on the platform’s self-service capabilities and overall ease of use. |\n| Level of Standardization | Consistency and reusability of platform services and workflows across teams. |\n| Service Create Time | Time required for a developer to create and deploy a new service using the platform. |\n| Autonomy Score | A measure inversely related to the number of support requests per year, reflecting developer independence. |\n\n### Detailed Explanations Self-Service Maturity\n\n* **Self-Service Adoption Rate**\n\n  + Measures the percentage of deployments and configurations completed without manual intervention.\n  + A high adoption rate indicates that the platform enables developers to work efficiently without heavy reliance on platform teams.\n  + Low adoption suggests gaps in documentation, usability, or platform capabilities.\n* **Time to Cloud**\n\n  + Measures how long it takes for a developer to go from an idea to a fully deployed service in the cloud.\n  + A lower time to cloud indicates a streamlined, efficient self-service experience.\n  + Captures the effectiveness of onboarding, provisioning, and deployment workflows.\n  + High time to cloud may signal bottlenecks in usability, approvals, or tooling gaps.\n* **Developer Satisfaction (Self-Service)**\n\n  + Gauges how satisfied developers are with the internal platform’s self-service capabilities and overall experience.\n  + High satisfaction indicates a developer-friendly, intuitive, and efficient platform.\n  + Low satisfaction may point to complexity, lack of documentation, or performance issues.\n* **Level of Standardization**\n\n  + Measures how consistent and
